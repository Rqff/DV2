Are we running out of time?

The big picture: why should we care?

In 2016, the Bramble Cay melomys, a small rodent from a sandbar on the Great Barrier Reef, was declared extinct after rising seas washed over its home. It was widely reported as the first mammal lost to human-caused climate change. This is the story of the species still standing where it stood.

Since European settlement, Australia has lost more mammal species than any other country. The survivors are not safe either. Look at where the threatened species cluster: Brazil, China and India stand out. Mass pollution from factories, habitat devastation to make space for agriculture and rubbish clogging the waters.

How bad is it?

Imagine walking through the bush and meeting 100 of the Australian species scientists are tracking. About 68 of them would be in trouble: vulnerable, endangered or critically endangered. More than two thirds of monitored species are struggling, where there is an equal amount of critically endangered species as there are species of least concern.

How many more have to go extinct before more is done about it?

Each individual species that is threatened is on display here. If nothing changes then it is very likely some of these dots will disappear in the next decade, and once they are gone they never come back. However particular groups struggle the most, like Small Mammals and Amphibians.

Amphibians soak up pollutants in the air and water around them, and they birth their young in the very same toxic sludge that is killing them.

Small mammals face introduced species that they never evolved to go up against, and as the invasive species wreak havoc through native populations the problem only accelerates.

Which animals are in the most trouble?

Looking globally we can see that the highest risk animal groups change. Fish have the most endangered and critically endangered species, followed closely by Amphibians, demonstrating the damaging trend to waterbound animals.

Birds have the most extinct species as a result of historical deforestation practices that have since been remedied.

The habitats that have the greatest biodiversity, Wetlands and Forests, also have the greatest number of species at risk.

They are also the most frequently polluted and decimated of any habitat thanks to modern human-introduced threats like: deforestation, freshwater pollution, and microplastics.

Habitats rich in variety of flora and fauna are the most fragile to damage from imbalance in the ecosystem. A species going extinct can have cascading effects on the whole biotic network.

Where do threatened animals live?

Looking at specific sub-categories of habitats the trend ecological damage can be better understood. The most bioavailable habitats consequently have the greatest span of species. Despite the abundance of resources that allowed these environments to flourish, they are at the most risk due to external factors.

Permanent Rivers, Streams and Creeks have the most endangered and critically endangered species of any habitat. They are incredibly dense with life as freshwater only accounts for 2% of the world's water, and yet has roughly the same amount of species as all of the world's oceans.

Zooming in: who is inside each group?

A threatened animal is rarely threatened alone, because its home is too. As animals' habitats are destroyed, their numbers freefall. And it also works in reverse, if an animal's count is dwindling it's often traced back to habitat devastation.

The connections between endangered, critically endangered and extinct species, their respective habitats, and their taxonomic group highlight the damage polluted rainforests and wetlands has on the Amphibian population.

Australia, state by state

So what does this mean for you? Every state and territory has threatened species in its backyard.

The Australian Capital Territory has the most when area-normalised as it shares common species with New South Wales and Victoria whilst having a tiny area of roughly 2,400 km².

For all the major states Victoria has a significant lead with the highest area-normalised incidence of threatened species. Victoria has one of the highest proportions of urbanised land, with a similar level of infrastructure to New South Wales despite being 3.5x smaller by area.

But are the urban areas where we see animals struggling?

Extinction rarely has a single villain. The most obvious contributing factor in Victoria is introduced predators like cats, foxes, and the infamous cane toad.

But there are less obvious factors such as the chytrid fungus that has devastated frogs.

However the factor that is a threat everywhere you go: climate change. There are more bushfires, droughts, floods and cyclones now than any other time in modern human history.

Threatened species are all across Victoria and it is because climate change does not discriminate, it affects everyone.

Are we watching closely enough?

You cannot protect what you do not count, and a species nobody is watching can slip away unnoticed. Animal studies are getting more frequent and more detailed every year. And now is the most we have ever known about the world and animals around us in any time in history.

The number of animal studies on species classified as critically endangered are far less than those of least concern. This is because they are easier to find and observe in the wild, but also because there are still new species being discovered everyday.

Who is bouncing back?

Extinction is not always the end of the story. Leadbeater's possum was written off as extinct for decades, until it turned up alive in Victoria in 1961. It is still critically endangered today.

Birds have seen the strongest return in numbers, whilst Amphibians are falling the most. We learned from losing so many Birds to extinction, hopefully we can learn for others too.

So, are we running out of time?

The honest answer is that for some species, unfortunately yes.

Amphibians are under the most pressure, and there are still gaps in what we know.

But the same data shows species recovering when people commit to protecting them

The melomys was gone before we could act. Most of the animals in these charts are not.
